Keyword Research for Beginners: How to Find Topics Worth Writing About

How to decide what to write about before you write it — using free tools, your own customer questions, and an honest look at who you're competing with.

Most new sites publish articles about whatever feels important to the owner, then wonder why nobody finds them. Keyword research is the step that connects what you know to what people are actually typing into a search box. It doesn't need expensive software — it needs a method, some patience, and a willingness to look at real search results instead of guessing.

What keyword research is actually for

A keyword is simply a phrase someone types (or says) when they want something. Research answers three practical questions before you spend hours on an article:

  • Is anyone looking for this? Some topics you care about have almost no search demand, and that's worth knowing early.
  • What do they want when they search it? The same words can mean very different things — see the intent section below.
  • Can you realistically compete? A new site cannot outrank established brands on broad terms, but it can win narrower ones.

Start with what you already know

The best seed keywords rarely come from a tool. They come from the questions real people already ask you: emails from customers, questions in sales calls, comments on social posts, and things you find yourself explaining repeatedly. Write these down in the customer's own words, not industry jargon. A plumber's customers search "why is my tap dripping", not "faucet cartridge failure".

Free ways to expand the list

Once you have ten or twenty seed topics, expand them with tools that cost nothing:

  • Google autocomplete: type a seed phrase and note the suggestions that appear. They come from real searches.
  • "People also ask" and "Related searches": found on results pages, these show how searchers phrase follow-up questions.
  • Google Search Console: once your site has some impressions, the Performance report shows queries you already appear for — often the best source of new ideas.
  • Google Keyword Planner: free with a Google Ads account. Volumes are shown as ranges unless you run ads, so treat them as a rough guide.
  • Google Trends: useful for comparing two phrasings and spotting seasonal patterns, and for checking interest by country or region.

Paid tools exist and can save time later, but a beginner gets most of the value from the free set above.

Understand search intent

Intent is the reason behind a search, and it decides what kind of page can rank. If the top results are all how-to guides and you publish a product page, you're unlikely to appear no matter how well you optimise.

Before committing to a keyword, search it yourself and look at the first page. Whatever format dominates is the format Google believes searchers want.

Why new sites should start with long-tail phrases

Long-tail keywords are longer, more specific phrases. "SEO" is dominated by giant publishers and agencies. "SEO checklist for a new restaurant website" is far narrower — fewer people search it, but the people who do have a clear need, and far fewer strong pages compete for it. A new site earns its first rankings on narrow topics, then builds the authority to tackle broader ones. Our SEO for Beginners guide covers the wider picture of how this fits together.

Judging competition honestly

Keyword "difficulty" scores from tools are estimates. A more reliable check is to read the actual first page and ask what you see:

Signal on page oneWhat it suggests
Mostly large brands and well-known publishersHard for a new site; consider a narrower variation
Forum threads, Q&A pages, or thin postsOpportunity — a genuinely helpful page can compete
Results that don't match the intent wellOpportunity to publish the format searchers are missing
Outdated articlesA current, well-structured guide has a fair chance

Build a simple keyword sheet

A spreadsheet is enough. Use these columns:

  1. Keyword or phrase — in the searcher's words.
  2. Intent — informational, commercial, transactional.
  3. Rough demand — low, medium, or high from the tools above.
  4. Competition — your own read of page one.
  5. Fit — how closely it matches something you can genuinely help with.
  6. Target page — the URL that will cover it, existing or planned.

Prioritise phrases that combine reasonable demand, beatable competition, and strong fit. Fit matters most: traffic that has nothing to do with what you offer doesn't help a business.

Map each keyword to one page

Give every important keyword a single home page and avoid writing two articles that answer the same question — they compete against each other and dilute both. Group closely related phrases together and cover them on one thorough page instead. Then link related pages to each other so search engines and readers can follow the path.

Regional and language angles

If you serve a specific country or city, add location modifiers ("in Karachi", "in Dubai", "for US small businesses") and check how results differ by location. In multilingual markets, people often search in a mix of English and a local language, or in transliterated words (Urdu written in Roman letters, for example). Try the variations your real customers use — they often show far less competition than the polished English equivalent.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Chasing volume only. A phrase with 50 searches from the right people beats one with 5,000 from the wrong ones.
  • Ignoring intent. Matching the words but not the format leaves you invisible.
  • Stuffing the keyword. Write naturally; search engines understand related phrases and synonyms.
  • Researching once and never again. Revisit your list every few months using Search Console data.

Conclusion

Good keyword research is less about tools and more about paying attention: to your customers' questions, to the search results themselves, and to the gap between what people want and what already exists. Start narrow, match intent, write one strong page per topic, and let real search data guide what you publish next.
