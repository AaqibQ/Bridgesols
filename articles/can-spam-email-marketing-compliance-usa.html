CAN-SPAM Compliance for US Email Marketing: A Practical Guide

Most US small businesses set up an email list and start sending without ever reading the law that governs it. CAN-SPAM is more permissive than people assume, but it has specific, non-negotiable requirements — and the Federal Trade Commission can fine up to $53,088 per violating email.

If you're a US small business sending marketing emails, the law that governs those emails is the CAN-SPAM Act (Controlling the Assault of Non-Solicited Pornography And Marketing Act), enforced by the Federal Trade Commission. It's worth understanding on its own merits, separate from any email platform's built-in compliance features, because the penalties are per-email, not per-campaign: as of the FTC's most recent inflation adjustment, violations can run up to $53,088 each. A single campaign sent to a list of a few thousand people with one missing requirement is not a hypothetical problem — it's a very real number. The good news is that CAN-SPAM is one of the more permissive commercial email laws in the world. It doesn't require advance opt-in consent the way the EU's GDPR or Canada's CASL do. What it requires is honesty and an easy way out, applied consistently. This guide covers exactly what that means in practice.

Who CAN-SPAM actually applies to

CAN-SPAM covers any email whose "primary purpose" is commercial advertising or promotion — a sale announcement, a discount code, a product launch email. It does not cover purely transactional or relationship emails: an order confirmation, a shipping notice, a password reset, or a reply to a customer service inquiry. The distinction matters because transactional emails are exempt from most of CAN-SPAM's requirements (though they still can't contain false or misleading routing information). The complication is mixed-content emails — a shipping confirmation that also pitches a discount on the next order. The FTC treats these based on what a reasonable recipient would interpret the email's primary purpose to be; if the promotional content dominates the email or the subject line emphasizes the offer over the transaction, it's treated as commercial and the full rules apply. When in doubt, send the promotional content as a separate email rather than bundling it into a transactional one.

The core requirements, in plain terms

Every commercial email you send has to satisfy all of the following. There's no partial credit — missing any one of these is a violation, independent of the others.

1. Accurate header information

The "From," "To," and routing information — including the domain name and email address — must identify the person or business who actually sent the message. You can't send from a domain or display name designed to disguise who's emailing, and you can't spoof a sender address that isn't yours. This is the easiest requirement to satisfy correctly and the one that causes the most legal trouble when ignored, because deceptive headers are treated as the most serious kind of violation.

2. Non-deceptive subject lines

The subject line can't misrepresent the content of the message. "Your order has shipped" when nothing shipped, or "RE: our conversation" when there was no prior conversation, both violate this requirement — regardless of how good the resulting open rate looks. This also covers subject lines that create false urgency about something that isn't actually true, like a fake "final notice" on an account that isn't actually expiring.

3. Disclosure that it's an advertisement

You have to clearly disclose, somewhere a recipient would notice, that the message is an advertisement or solicitation. For most small business emails this is satisfied implicitly by obviously promotional content and context — a newsletter that's clearly marketing isn't required to add the literal word "advertisement" — but if the email's commercial nature is at all ambiguous, make it explicit rather than relying on interpretation.

4. A valid physical postal address

Every commercial email must include your business's valid physical postal address. This can be your actual street address, a registered post office box, or a commercial mail receiving agency address obtained under USPS regulations. A home-based business that doesn't want to publish a home address should register a PO box or use a virtual mailbox service rather than skip this requirement — it's non-negotiable, and most email platforms won't let a campaign send without one populated in the footer.

5. A clear and conspicuous opt-out mechanism

Every commercial email needs a visible, easy-to-find way to opt out of future emails — typically an "unsubscribe" link in the footer. It can't be hidden in small gray text that's technically present but practically invisible, and you can't require the recipient to do anything beyond provide their email address and click to opt out (no login requirement, no "tell us why you're leaving" mandatory survey, no fee).

6. Honoring opt-outs within 10 business days

Once someone opts out, you have 10 business days to stop sending them commercial email — and after that, you can never sell, transfer, or share their address with anyone else for the purpose of sending them commercial email. Most email platforms process opt-outs automatically within minutes, but if you're managing lists manually or importing contacts between tools, this is where compliance commonly breaks: a contact unsubscribes in one system but gets re-added through an import from another.

7. Monitoring what others do on your behalf

If you hire an agency, freelancer, or affiliate to send email marketing for you, you're still legally responsible for their compliance with all of the above. "Our email vendor handled that" is not a defense the FTC accepts — you have to actually verify that whoever sends on your behalf is following the rules, not just assume it.

What CAN-SPAM does not require

This is where a lot of US small businesses over-comply out of caution, sometimes at the cost of list growth they didn't need to sacrifice. CAN-SPAM, unlike GDPR or CASL, does not require:

  • Advance opt-in consent before the first email. You can legally email someone without prior permission, as long as every other requirement is met — though in practice, cold-emailing uninvited lists tanks deliverability and spam complaint rates fast enough that it's rarely worth doing regardless of legality.
  • A double opt-in confirmation step. Single opt-in (one signup action, no confirmation email required) is legally sufficient, though double opt-in is still a smart deliverability practice for other reasons.
  • A specific record-retention period for consent. Unlike TCPA for SMS, CAN-SPAM doesn't mandate keeping a timestamped consent log, since consent generally isn't the legal gate in the first place.

If you sell into Canada or the EU in addition to the US, note that this permissiveness is US-specific — CASL and GDPR both require actual opt-in consent before a first commercial email, and a US business emailing contacts in those jurisdictions is generally expected to meet the stricter standard for those recipients.

Setting this up correctly from the start

  1. Pick an email platform that enforces the basics for you. Mainstream platforms (Mailchimp, Klaviyo, Constant Contact, and similar) require a populated physical address and auto-insert an unsubscribe link in every campaign — they won't let you send without both.
  2. Use a recognizable sending domain and display name. Authenticate your sending domain with SPF, DKIM, and DMARC — this isn't a CAN-SPAM requirement on paper, but it reinforces the "accurate header" requirement and dramatically improves inbox placement.
  3. Register a PO box if you work from home and don't want your home address published. This typically costs $60–$300/year depending on location and box size.
  4. Write subject lines that describe the actual content, and review them against the email body before sending — a quick two-minute check that prevents the most common deceptive-subject-line violations.
  5. Test your own unsubscribe link every few campaigns. Click it yourself periodically to confirm it still works after any platform update or template change.
  6. Keep your contact list synced across tools. If you use separate platforms for email, SMS, and your CRM, build a process (even a manual monthly export/import) that propagates opt-outs everywhere, not just the platform the person unsubscribed from.

What enforcement actually looks like

In practice, the FTC rarely pursues a small business over a single email with one missing footer address — enforcement tends to target larger-scale, clearly deceptive campaigns (fake subject lines at volume, deliberately disguised senders, ignored opt-outs at scale) rather than isolated technical slips by a legitimate local business. That said, "rarely enforced against small businesses" isn't the same as "safe to ignore," for two reasons. First, state attorneys general and private plaintiffs can also bring CAN-SPAM claims in some circumstances, widening who can act on a violation. Second, and more immediately, the email platforms themselves (and the mailbox providers like Gmail and Outlook receiving your mail) enforce stricter versions of these same norms through spam filtering and account suspension — a business that sends deceptive subject lines or ignores opt-outs will usually get throttled or banned by its own email platform long before the FTC ever gets involved.

Mistakes that cause the most trouble

  • Treating transactional emails as a loophole for promotional content. Stuffing a discount code into an order confirmation doesn't exempt it from the commercial-email rules if promotion is what the email is really about.
  • Letting an old contact list override recent opt-outs. Re-uploading a spreadsheet of contacts without cross-checking it against your current suppression list reintroduces people who already unsubscribed.
  • Buying or renting email lists. Even where technically not a direct CAN-SPAM violation on its own, purchased lists generate spam complaints and bounce rates that get sending domains blacklisted — a self-inflicted wound that's often worse than the legal risk.
  • Assuming a privacy policy substitutes for an opt-out link. They serve different legal purposes; a privacy policy doesn't satisfy the per-email opt-out requirement.
  • Ignoring what an agency or affiliate sends under your brand. You're on the hook for their compliance, so a quarterly spot-check of what's actually going out under your name is worth the ten minutes it takes.

Conclusion

CAN-SPAM asks less of a US small business than most people assume — no advance consent, no double opt-in, no mandatory consent logs — but what it does ask for is specific and has to be present in every single commercial email: an honest sender and subject line, a real physical address, and an opt-out that actually works within 10 days. None of it requires a lawyer or a compliance platform to get right; it requires picking an email tool that enforces the basics, writing honestly, and keeping your suppression list in sync across whatever tools touch your contacts. Get that in place once and it stays compliant by default.
