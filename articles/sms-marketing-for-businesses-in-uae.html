SMS Marketing for Businesses in the UAE: A Practical Guide to TDRA Compliance and Bulk SMS Registration

A business in the US can sign up with almost any SMS platform and start texting once a number is registered. In the UAE, the starting point is different: you generally can't send bulk commercial SMS at all unless the message passes through a TDRA-licensed channel. Here's how the system actually works.

SMS still earns a place in the UAE marketing mix that WhatsApp hasn't fully replaced — delivery confirmations, appointment reminders, OTP codes, and time-sensitive offers still land as texts because they don't depend on the recipient having WhatsApp installed, data turned on, or your business number saved as a contact. But the UAE regulates commercial SMS more tightly, and differently, than most businesses expect coming from a US or UK playbook. The core difference: in the US, a business (or its SMS platform) registers its own sending number through the carriers and starts sending. In the UAE, the Telecommunications and Digital Government Regulatory Authority (TDRA) treats bulk commercial messaging as a licensed activity in its own right, and most businesses never hold that license themselves — they send through an aggregator who does. Get that structure wrong and messages don't quietly underperform; they get blocked at the network level before they reach anyone.

Why SMS still earns a spot next to WhatsApp

The UAE has one of the highest smartphone and WhatsApp adoption rates in the world, and for most ongoing customer conversation — order updates, support, catalogs — WhatsApp Business is the better tool (BridgeSols has a separate guide to WhatsApp Business for sales that covers that channel). SMS keeps three advantages WhatsApp doesn't fully replace: it reaches a phone number with zero setup on the recipient's side, it's the default channel for one-time passwords and account security alerts that people expect from a bank or delivery app, and it still reads as more "official" for time-critical logistics messages (a flight delay, a delivery arriving in the next hour) than a WhatsApp notification that can get lost in a busy chat list. For most UAE businesses, the realistic strategy is WhatsApp for ongoing conversation and SMS for the narrow set of messages that need to land regardless of app state — not a wholesale SMS marketing program with frequent promotional blasts.

The TDRA's unsolicited communications framework

The TDRA (the regulator that replaced the standalone TRA, now operating alongside its digital government mandate) regulates unsolicited commercial electronic communications — SMS, email, fax, and recorded voice calls — under a long-standing policy that requires prior consent before a business sends marketing content to a UAE number, and mandates a working opt-out in every message. That consent requirement runs in parallel with the UAE's Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL), which governs how phone numbers are collected and stored as personal data in the first place; BridgeSols' guide to UAE email marketing compliance covers the PDPL side in more depth, since the consent and data-handling principles largely carry over from email to SMS. What's specific to SMS is the delivery layer: the TDRA doesn't just regulate what you're allowed to send, it regulates who is allowed to inject bulk SMS traffic into the UAE's mobile networks at all.

Who can actually send bulk commercial SMS

This is the step that catches businesses off guard. In most Western markets, any business can sign up with a platform like Twilio or SimpleTexting, register a number, and start sending at volume once the carrier paperwork clears. In the UAE, bulk commercial SMS traffic has to be injected through a channel that both of the licensed telecom operators — Etisalat by e& and du — and the TDRA recognize as an approved bulk messaging source. In practice, that means:

  • Going through a TDRA-registered bulk SMS aggregator. These are companies whose entire business is holding the regulatory relationships and network connections needed to send bulk SMS legally, and they resell that capacity to businesses as a platform or API. Most UAE businesses never apply for sending rights directly — they sign up with an aggregator the same way a US business signs up with an SMS platform, except the aggregator's TDRA registration is doing legal work a US platform doesn't need to do.
  • Sending through your mobile operator's own business messaging product. Etisalat and du both offer bulk SMS and business messaging products directly to corporate customers, which is an option for larger businesses that want a direct operator relationship instead of a third-party aggregator.
  • Never sending bulk promotional SMS from a regular SIM or business number. A personal or standard business mobile line is provisioned for person-to-person traffic. Sending marketing blasts from it at volume typically gets flagged and throttled by the operator's own anti-spam systems well before any regulatory action — it simply isn't the product that line is sold as.

Before signing anything, ask a prospective SMS provider directly whether they are a TDRA-registered bulk messaging aggregator or operate under one, and ask for that registration to be stated in writing. A platform that can't answer that question clearly, or that suggests sending bulk marketing texts from a regular number, isn't a safe choice for the UAE market even if it works fine for clients elsewhere.

Sender ID registration: the UAE's version of a 10DLC campaign

Once you have an aggregator or operator relationship, the next step is registering a Sender ID — the alphanumeric name (e.g., your brand name, up to 11 characters, letters and numbers only) that appears as the sender instead of a phone number. Unlike a numeric long code, an alphanumeric Sender ID can't receive replies, but it reads as more legitimate and is the standard format for UAE commercial SMS. Registering one typically involves:

  1. Submitting your business's trade license and the requested Sender ID to your aggregator or operator, who forwards it for approval.
  2. Approval by both mobile operators — a Sender ID approved for Etisalat traffic isn't automatically valid on du, and vice versa; most aggregators handle both submissions as a package, but confirm this rather than assuming it.
  3. Matching the Sender ID to your registered trade name. Operators reject Sender IDs that don't reasonably match the legal or trading name on file, partly as an anti-impersonation safeguard — you can't register a generic or unrelated-sounding ID and expect it to pass review.

Approval generally takes from a few business days to a couple of weeks depending on the aggregator's relationship with each operator and how complete the submitted documentation is. Start this well ahead of a planned campaign — a Sender ID held up in review is the single most common reason a UAE SMS launch slips its date.

Getting consent that actually holds up

Consent requirements under the TDRA's unsolicited communications policy and the PDPL both point the same direction: a phone number collected for one purpose — an order confirmation, a support ticket, a loyalty sign-up at checkout — isn't automatically usable for promotional SMS. Valid consent needs a clear, affirmative opt-in that discloses the customer will receive marketing messages, ideally captured at the same moment as the phone number itself (a checkbox on a web form, an explicit opt-in question at checkout, a keyword reply). Keep a timestamped record of how and when each number opted in; if a customer ever disputes receiving unsolicited messages, that record is the entire defense. Buying or renting a contact list — a practice that still circulates informally in some UAE SME circles — carries real regulatory risk and almost never comes with consent that would survive scrutiny, regardless of what the seller claims.

Choosing a UAE-ready SMS provider

Beyond confirming TDRA registration, evaluate a provider on: AED billing (avoids repeated currency conversion fees on every top-up), direct connectivity to both Etisalat and du rather than routing through a third country (which adds latency and failure points), Arabic-language support in the sending platform and delivery reports, documented Sender ID turnaround times, and transparent per-message pricing broken out from any monthly platform fee. If you already use a UAE-based CRM or marketing platform, check whether it has a built-in UAE SMS module through an established aggregator partnership before adding a separate standalone tool — keeping contact and consent records in one system avoids the same sync problems that cause compliance gaps between channels.

What to actually send

Transactional and service messages

OTPs, order confirmations, delivery updates, and appointment reminders generally sit outside strict promotional consent rules when they're purely informational and tied to a transaction the customer initiated. The moment a delivery update includes a promotional line ("and here's 20% off your next order"), it becomes a marketing message in substance and should be treated as one — kept to its own registered use case with its own consent trail, not folded into transactional traffic.

Promotional campaigns

Seasonal sales, new product launches, and limited-time offers. Keep these infrequent — a few times a month is a reasonable ceiling — and be deliberate about timing around Ramadan and other periods when message volume across the market spikes and recipient tolerance for interruption drops; sending during late evening or very early morning hours is both poor practice and more likely to generate complaints that get reported to the operator or TDRA.

Loyalty and reminder flows

For retail and hospitality businesses, a short reminder about an expiring loyalty reward or an abandoned booking performs reliably as an automated flow, since it's tied to something the customer already engaged with rather than a cold promotional push.

Writing texts for a bilingual audience

  • Identify the business by name in the message itself — an alphanumeric Sender ID helps, but the first line should still make the sender obvious without the recipient needing to recognize the ID.
  • Decide deliberately between English, Arabic, or both. A single bilingual message (English line, Arabic line) works for a genuinely mixed audience; forcing both into every message to every segment usually just doubles the character count and cost without adding clarity. Segment by customer-recorded language preference where you can.
  • Keep messages within a single SMS segment where possible — Arabic text uses Unicode encoding, which cuts the per-segment character limit roughly in half compared to English (about 70 characters versus 160), so Arabic messages split into multiple billed segments sooner than English ones of similar length.
  • Always include a clear opt-out instruction in whichever language the message is sent in.
  • One link, one offer — the same discipline that applies to any SMS campaign applies here regardless of language.

Measuring what's working

Track delivery rate, opt-out rate, and link click-through per campaign through your aggregator's reporting dashboard, and compare delivery rates across Etisalat and du separately rather than as a blended average — a drop specific to one operator usually points to a Sender ID or routing issue with that network rather than a content problem. Watch opt-out rate the same way you would anywhere else: a sustained spike signals the message frequency or relevance has drifted from what people opted in for, independent of whether the campaign is technically compliant.

Mistakes that cause the most trouble

  • Assuming an international SMS platform handles UAE compliance by default. Many global platforms can technically send to UAE numbers but aren't TDRA-registered aggregators; confirm this explicitly rather than assuming coverage.
  • Trying to send bulk marketing texts from a standard business SIM. This gets throttled by the operator's own systems and doesn't scale past a handful of messages.
  • Registering a Sender ID with only one operator. Messages land for half your audience and silently fail for the rest, with no obvious error to explain the gap.
  • Treating a number collected for delivery updates as fair game for promotions. The consent doesn't transfer automatically, under either the TDRA framework or the PDPL.
  • Ignoring the cost difference of Arabic-language messages. Budgeting for English-length segments while sending Arabic content leads to surprise overage costs at scale.

Conclusion

SMS marketing in the UAE rewards businesses that treat the regulatory structure as the starting point, not an afterthought. Confirm your provider is actually a TDRA-registered aggregator or operator channel, register an alphanumeric Sender ID with both Etisalat and du before you need it, collect and document consent properly, and keep promotional volume low enough that a channel with near-universal reach stays one people want to keep receiving.
