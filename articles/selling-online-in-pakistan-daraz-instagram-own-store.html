Selling Online in Pakistan: Daraz, Instagram, or Your Own Store

Most sellers in Pakistan start on Daraz or Instagram because it's fast and free to set up — but each comes with trade-offs that only show up once orders start arriving. Here's how to choose, and how to combine them as you grow.

If you're selling a physical product in Pakistan — clothing, skincare, home goods, accessories — you have three realistic starting points: list on a marketplace like Daraz, sell directly through Instagram or Facebook, or build your own store. Most successful sellers eventually use more than one, but you still have to pick where to start, and that choice shapes your margins, your cash flow, and how much control you have over your customers. This guide walks through each option honestly, including the parts sellers usually only learn after their first few hundred orders.

The three options, briefly

Daraz is Pakistan's largest general marketplace. You list products into an existing audience that's already there to buy, with built-in payment collection and a logistics network. You pay for that convenience in commission and in not owning the customer relationship. Instagram and Facebook shops (run directly through DMs, WhatsApp, or a simple catalog) let you sell to an audience you build yourself, with far more control over presentation and pricing, but you handle payments, order tracking, and delivery arrangements manually or semi-manually. Your own store (built on Shopify, WooCommerce, or a similar platform) gives you full control and your own customer data, but it comes with upfront setup, a learning curve, and — critically — the need to drive your own traffic since no one is browsing a blank store the way they browse Daraz or Instagram.

Starting on Daraz: what actually happens

Daraz works well for products that benefit from search and browse discovery — things people actively look for, like phone accessories, kitchenware, or basic clothing. To sell, you register as a seller, get verified (expect to submit a CNIC and bank details), and list products with photos, variants, and pricing. Daraz handles payment collection, including cash on delivery, and routes the order to its own delivery network or your pickup point.

The trade-off is visibility and margin. Your listing sits next to dozens of similar ones, often competing mainly on price, and Daraz's commission plus any fulfillment or advertising fees you choose to pay can take a meaningful cut before you see your margin. Returns and COD refusal rates — a real issue in Pakistan's COD-heavy market — also eat into revenue in ways that are easy to underestimate when you're only looking at the sale price.

Daraz is a reasonable place to test demand for a new product quickly, since the audience is already there. It's a weaker place to build a brand, since customers associate the purchase with Daraz, not with you.

Selling directly on Instagram and Facebook

This is how a large share of small sellers in Pakistan actually operate: product photos and reels on Instagram, inquiries through DMs or WhatsApp, and payment by bank transfer, JazzCash, Easypaisa, or cash on delivery arranged directly with a courier. Our WhatsApp Business guide covers setting up catalogs and quick replies for this exact workflow.

The appeal is control — you set the price, write the caption, and build a direct relationship with buyers who can message you back. The cost is that none of it is automated. Every order typically means a manual conversation, a manual payment confirmation, and a manual booking with a courier (common choices include TCS, Leopards, and M&P, depending on your city and volume). This works fine at low volume and becomes a real bottleneck once you're processing dozens of orders a day — at that point, sellers usually start looking at a lightweight order-tracking sheet or a small store to reduce the manual load.

A Facebook or Instagram "Shop" catalog can reduce some of the DM back-and-forth by letting people browse products in-app, but checkout in Pakistan still largely routes through direct messages and manual payment, since in-app checkout support for local payment methods is limited.

Building your own store

A store on Shopify, WooCommerce (on WordPress), or a similar platform makes sense once you have a repeatable product line and want to stop depending entirely on marketplace or social traffic. It gives you a real checkout, order history, and the ability to run your own promotions without a platform's rules in the way.

The catch sellers underestimate: a store doesn't generate its own traffic. Unlike Daraz, nobody is browsing your store by default — you need SEO, paid ads, social traffic, or an existing audience to send people to it. On the payments side, check which gateways actually support Pakistani merchant accounts before committing to a platform — availability and requirements for local payment gateways change, so verify current options (and whether you'll need a registered business to open a merchant account) directly with the provider rather than relying on older guides.

A realistic path: combine them instead of picking one forever

  1. Validate with Instagram or Daraz first. Low setup cost, fast feedback on whether people actually want the product.
  2. Use Daraz to test demand for new product lines where search-based discovery helps, while keeping your core brand sales on channels you control.
  3. Build an Instagram following and capture contacts (WhatsApp number or email) from every sale, so you're not fully dependent on any single platform's algorithm or fee structure.
  4. Move your most loyal, repeat buyers to your own store once you have enough volume to justify the setup — this is where margins improve, since there's no marketplace commission.
  5. Keep Daraz or marketplace listings running for new-customer acquisition even after your own store is live; the two serve different jobs.

Very few sellers in Pakistan succeed on a single channel forever. The ones who grow past a certain size usually run two or three channels at once, each doing a different job — discovery, relationship-building, and margin-protected direct sales.

Delivery and cash-on-delivery, honestly

Cash on delivery remains dominant for a large share of online orders in Pakistan, and it comes with a real cost: a meaningful share of COD orders get refused at the door, especially from new or unverified customers. This isn't unique to any one channel — it affects Daraz sellers, Instagram sellers, and store owners alike. Practical ways sellers reduce it include calling to confirm orders above a certain value before dispatch, offering a small discount for prepaid orders, and tracking refusal rates by courier and by customer so repeat offenders can be filtered out over time. Budget for this cost explicitly rather than discovering it in your first month's numbers.

Pricing across channels without undercutting yourself

A common mistake is pricing the same product differently across Daraz, Instagram, and your own store without accounting for each channel's costs — then wondering why the marketplace version, which carries a commission, isn't actually profitable at the same price point. Calculate your true per-channel margin (product cost, packaging, delivery, payment fees, and commission where it applies) before setting a price, and keep prices consistent enough across channels that customers don't feel like they're being charged differently for no reason.

Registration, invoicing, and taxes

Many sellers start informally — a personal Instagram account and a personal bank account — and that's a reasonable way to validate a product. Once sales become steady, a few practical things become worth sorting out rather than putting off indefinitely. Daraz and most payment gateways will eventually ask for business or NTN (National Tax Number) details to settle larger payment volumes, and operating without any registration can make it harder to open a proper merchant account or dispute a payment issue later. Registering as a sole proprietor is usually the simplest first step, and it's worth a short conversation with a local accountant about when you cross into needing to file sales tax or income tax on the business, since the rules and thresholds change and vary by province. None of this should block you from starting — it becomes relevant once there's real, recurring revenue to protect.

Keep basic records from day one regardless: a simple spreadsheet of orders, costs, and channel is enough early on, and it saves a lot of pain later when it's time to register or to figure out which channel is actually profitable.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Treating Daraz commission as an afterthought instead of pricing it in from day one.
  • Never capturing customer contact details on Instagram sales, leaving you unable to re-market to past buyers.
  • Building a full store before validating demand for the product itself.
  • Ignoring COD refusal rates until they've quietly eaten a month's profit.
  • Relying on one channel entirely, so a policy change or algorithm shift can stop sales overnight.

Conclusion

Daraz, Instagram, and your own store each solve a different problem — discovery, direct relationships, and margin-protected sales, respectively. Most sellers in Pakistan start on whichever is fastest to set up, then add the others as volume grows. Price honestly for each channel's real costs, capture customer contacts wherever you can, and expect to run more than one channel at once rather than searching for the single "best" one.
