Dropshipping: A Practical Guide to Starting an Online Store Without Holding Inventory

How dropshipping actually works, picking a supplier and niche that can survive on real margins, and the fulfillment and customer-service problems that sink most stores before they turn a profit.

BE BridgeSols Editorial · Published October 21, 2026 · 13 min read

Dropshipping gets pitched as a way to run an online store without ever touching a product: a customer orders from your site, you forward that order to a supplier, and the supplier ships it directly to the customer. You never hold inventory, never pack a box, and never front the cost of stock. That part is true, and it's the reason dropshipping remains one of the lowest-capital ways to test whether a product can sell online. What the pitch usually leaves out is that the model trades inventory risk for thinner margins, slower shipping, and almost no control over the parts of the order experience customers actually judge you on — and most stores that fail do so for exactly those reasons, not because the idea was flawed.

How dropshipping actually works

The mechanics are simple. You list a product on your own storefront (commonly a Shopify store, though WooCommerce and other platforms work too) at a retail price you set. When a customer buys, you pay the supplier's wholesale price for that item and pass along the shipping address. The supplier — not you — picks, packs, and ships it, usually with their own packaging rather than yours. You collect the difference between what the customer paid and what you paid the supplier, minus payment processing fees, ad spend, and any app or platform costs.

Two supplier models dominate right now. The first is marketplace-style sourcing, where tools like DSers or AutoDS connect your store to suppliers (often based in China) found on AliExpress or similar marketplaces, and orders are routed automatically with little negotiation. The second is dedicated dropship suppliers — CJdropshipping, Spocket, Zendrop, and similar platforms — that offer faster shipping, sometimes US- or EU-based warehouses, and in some cases custom branding (private-label packaging and inserts) once you're doing enough volume to negotiate it. The second model generally costs more per unit but produces a materially better customer experience, which matters more than most new sellers expect.

Choosing a niche and a product that can actually hold margin

The products that look easiest to dropship — generic gadgets, novelty items, anything you've seen in a viral ad — are usually the worst choices, because thousands of other stores are sourcing the identical item from the identical supplier and competing purely on price and ad spend. A product is worth building a store around when at least one of these is true: it solves a specific, describable problem for a specific audience; it's difficult to find at a normal retail store or search easily on Amazon; or it benefits from some context, demonstration, or styling that a generic marketplace listing doesn't provide and your content can supply.

Beyond that, run every candidate product through a short set of practical filters before listing it:

  • Landed cost math works at a real ad cost. If the supplier price is $8 and shipping is $4, you need enough margin above $12 to cover a realistic cost-per-acquisition, not just the item's own cost.
  • It's not fragile, oversized, or battery-restricted. Anything that breaks easily, needs special handling, or trips airline battery rules adds returns, delays, and customs friction that erode margin fast.
  • Shipping time is honestly tolerable. If the realistic delivery window is 3–4 weeks, the product and the price point need to be forgiving of that — impulse-buy items under $15 tolerate long shipping far worse than a $60 purchase someone researched first.
  • You can picture the return or complaint. If a customer is likely to be disappointed by color, sizing, or quality versus the photos, that's a support and refund cost you're signing up for at scale.

The margin math nobody shows in the screenshots

Store the full cost stack, not just the supplier price, before deciding a product is viable. A realistic breakdown for a $30 retail item sourced at $9 looks like this:

Line itemTypical cost
Retail price$30.00
Supplier cost (item + shipping)−$11.50
Payment processing (~3%)−$0.90
Platform/app fees (prorated)−$0.60
Customer acquisition cost (ads)−$9.00–$14.00
Remaining margin before overhead$3.00–$8.00

That last row is the number that matters, and it's the one most new sellers never calculate before launching ads. A 60–70% markup over supplier cost sounds healthy until a realistic cost-per-acquisition is subtracted from it — which is exactly why products priced under roughly $25–$30 retail struggle to survive on paid traffic alone, and why experienced dropshippers either push average order value up (bundles, upsells, a second item added at checkout) or lean on organic and repeat traffic instead of buying every sale.

Setting up the store

  1. Pick a platform. Shopify is the default for a reason — its app ecosystem (DSers, Zendrop, review apps, upsell apps) is built specifically around dropshipping workflows. WooCommerce is viable if you already run WordPress and want more control, at the cost of more manual setup.
  2. Connect a supplier integration so orders sync automatically instead of requiring you to manually place each order with the supplier — manual order entry doesn't scale past a handful of sales a day and introduces costly mistakes.
  3. Write real product pages. Don't copy the supplier's listing photos and description verbatim — rewrite the copy, source or shoot better images where possible, and add the specific details (sizing, materials, what it does and doesn't solve) that a thin marketplace listing leaves out.
  4. Set a realistic shipping expectation on the page itself. State the actual delivery window rather than hiding it; an unpleasant surprise at checkout becomes a chargeback later, while an honest expectation set up front rarely does.
  5. Configure tax and business basics before taking real orders — a payment processor, a registered business entity if your market requires one for the volume you expect, and a returns/refund policy page that matches what your supplier will actually support.

Fulfillment, shipping time, and the support load you're actually signing up for

The single biggest gap between the dropshipping pitch and dropshipping reality is customer service. You don't control packing, so you can't fix a wrong item before it ships. You don't control the carrier, so you can't speed up a delayed package. And you don't control quality, so a batch of defective units from the supplier becomes your refund problem, not theirs. Budget real time — not an afterthought — for order tracking follow-up, "where is my order" messages, and negotiating refunds or reships with the supplier when something goes wrong, because something will go wrong on a regular basis at any meaningful order volume.

Set supplier expectations explicitly before relying on them: confirmed processing time before an order even ships, a tracked shipping method (untracked shipping creates disputes you can't resolve), and a clear return/defect policy you can forward to your own customers. Suppliers who won't commit to any of these in writing are a sign to keep testing other suppliers before scaling spend on that product.

Mistakes that sink most dropshipping stores

  • Launching paid ads before the margin math is checked. Spending on traffic before confirming the product can be profitable at a realistic cost-per-acquisition burns cash finding out the hard way.
  • Picking a product purely because an ad for it went viral. By the time a product is visibly trending in ad libraries, saturation and rising ad costs have usually already started.
  • Ignoring shipping time until customers complain. A 3-week delivery window hidden at checkout generates chargebacks and platform strikes that a disclosed window mostly avoids.
  • Treating the supplier relationship as disposable. Switching suppliers constantly to chase the lowest unit price makes it impossible to negotiate better terms, faster shipping, or custom packaging as volume grows.
  • No plan for returns or defects. A refund policy that promises more than the supplier will actually support turns every defect into money lost twice — once on the refund, once on the ad spend that acquired the customer.
  • Never testing a second or third product. A single-product store with no way to increase average order value caps growth at exactly the ad-cost ceiling described above.

When dropshipping makes sense — and when it doesn't

Dropshipping is a reasonable way to validate demand for a product idea cheaply before committing capital to inventory, and it can work as a lasting model for low-shipping-sensitivity niche products with real content or audience behind them. It's a poor fit as a long-term strategy for commodity products competing purely on price, for anything where shipping speed is a core part of the customer's expectation, or as a get-rich-quick shortcut — the margin math above applies whether or not a course seller says otherwise. Most sustainable dropshipping businesses eventually migrate their best-selling products to a small-batch or private-label inventory model once sales volume justifies the better margins and faster shipping that holding stock allows.

Conclusion

Dropshipping removes the inventory risk from starting an online store, but it doesn't remove the business risk — margin, shipping time, and customer service are still yours to manage, and suppliers who control fulfillment will never care about your refund rate as much as you do. Run the real margin math before spending on ads, choose a product and supplier that can survive honest shipping expectations, and treat it as a way to validate a product cheaply rather than a shortcut around running an actual business.
