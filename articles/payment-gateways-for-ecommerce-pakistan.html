Payment Gateways for E-Commerce in Pakistan: JazzCash, Easypaisa, and Bank Integration Compared

Cash on delivery still dominates online orders in Pakistan, but it caps how fast a store can grow and how much of each sale actually survives to profit. Here's how JazzCash, Easypaisa, and bank-based gateways actually work, what they cost, and how to combine them with COD instead of picking one over the other.

Most online sellers in Pakistan start with cash on delivery because it's the path of least resistance — no merchant account, no integration, no waiting on a bank's approval process. It also caps growth in ways that only become obvious once order volume rises: COD refusal rates eat margin, cash reconciliation eats time, and customers who would happily pay upfront for a discount or faster dispatch have no way to do it. Adding a real payment gateway solves that, but "add a payment gateway" in Pakistan isn't a single decision — it means choosing between mobile wallets, bank-run gateways, and international processors that each serve a different kind of business. This guide walks through what each option actually requires, what it costs, and how to combine digital payments with COD instead of replacing it outright.

The payment landscape, honestly

Three groups of options exist for a business selling online in Pakistan. Mobile wallet gateways — JazzCash and Easypaisa — are built around the mobile wallet accounts that a large share of the population already uses for bill payments and transfers, and they offer merchant checkout products on top of that base. Bank-based gateways, built on the 1LINK switch that connects Pakistani banks, let customers pay with a debit or credit card issued by a local bank, and are usually sold through a specific bank's merchant services team (Bank Alfalah's and HBL's PayFast products are the most commonly cited examples). International processors such as Payoneer or 2Checkout exist mainly for businesses billing customers outside Pakistan in foreign currency, since direct support for Pakistani merchants from the largest global gateways (Stripe, for instance) has historically been limited — confirm current availability directly with the provider rather than assuming it from an older guide, since coverage changes.

None of these fully replace cash on delivery for most consumer sellers today. The realistic goal is adding one digital option alongside COD, not eliminating COD on day one.

Setting up JazzCash for a business

JazzCash offers merchant products aimed at different business sizes, from a simple payment link or checkout page for a small store to a full API integration for a custom website or app. For most small e-commerce sellers, the practical starting point is a hosted checkout page — customers are redirected to a JazzCash-hosted payment screen, complete the transaction there, and are returned to the store's confirmation page. This avoids handling any payment credentials directly and is usually faster to get approved than a full custom API integration.

Onboarding typically asks for a registered business (a sole proprietorship is often enough to start), a business bank account for settlement, and basic tax registration (an NTN). Expect a verification step before the merchant account goes live — build that lead time into a launch date rather than assuming same-day activation.

Setting up Easypaisa for a business

Easypaisa's merchant onboarding follows a similar shape to JazzCash — a business registration and bank account, a verification step, and a choice between a hosted checkout and a deeper API integration for custom platforms. The main reason to offer Easypaisa alongside or instead of JazzCash isn't technical; it's customer preference. The two wallets have different user bases concentrated in different regions and demographics, and a store that only offers one will lose some checkout conversions simply because a customer's preferred wallet isn't an option. Many growing Pakistani stores eventually integrate both rather than choosing one permanently.

Bank-based gateways: broader card coverage

A bank-based gateway routes payments through the 1LINK network that connects Pakistani banks, letting a customer pay with practically any local debit or credit card rather than being limited to a specific wallet's user base. This is useful for stores selling higher-ticket items where the buyer is more likely to pay by card than by mobile wallet balance, or for B2B sellers whose customers are businesses rather than individual consumers.

The trade-off is process: these merchant accounts are typically arranged directly with a bank's merchant services or cash management team rather than through a simple online sign-up, and approval can take longer — plan for several weeks rather than a few days, and ask the bank directly for its current documentation requirements and timeline rather than assuming it matches a wallet provider's process.

Choosing how to integrate: hosted checkout, plugin, or API

  • Hosted checkout / payment link. The fastest to launch — customers are redirected to the gateway's own payment page. Good for a store that just needs payments working, without custom checkout design.
  • Platform plugin. If the store runs on WooCommerce, Shopify, or a similar platform, check first whether a maintained plugin exists for the chosen gateway — this handles most of the integration work without custom code, though plugin quality and maintenance vary, so test thoroughly before relying on one in production.
  • Direct API integration. Needed for a fully custom checkout experience or a mobile app. This requires development resources and more thorough testing, including every edge case (failed payments, timeouts, duplicate submissions) before going live.

For a first digital payment option, a hosted checkout or an existing plugin is almost always the right starting point. Move to a custom API integration only once volume justifies the extra development and testing effort.

A realistic integration path

  1. Register the business formally if it isn't already — most gateways require at minimum a sole proprietorship with an NTN before they'll open a merchant account.
  2. Open or confirm a business bank account that the gateway will settle funds into.
  3. Apply to one gateway first rather than several at once — this keeps the verification and testing load manageable and lets you learn the process before adding a second provider.
  4. Request sandbox/test credentials once approved, and run full test transactions — a successful payment, a failed one, and a refund — before touching the live environment.
  5. Integrate via the simplest method that fits the platform (plugin or hosted checkout before custom API), and test the entire checkout flow on both desktop and mobile.
  6. Go live with a small batch of real transactions first and confirm settlement actually arrives in the bank account on the timeline the gateway quoted before promoting the new payment option broadly.
  7. Add a second gateway (the other wallet, or a bank-based option) once the first is stable and volume justifies the extra integration and reconciliation work.

Fees and settlement, and why they matter to pricing

Every gateway charges a transaction fee, usually a percentage of the transaction value, and some add a fixed component or tiered pricing based on volume. These rates differ by provider, by business category, and by negotiated volume, and they're revised periodically — get the current fee schedule in writing from the gateway or bank rather than relying on a number quoted in another article. Settlement timing (how long after a transaction the funds actually land in the business bank account) also varies, commonly ranging from next-day to several business days depending on the provider and account tier.

Build the fee into product pricing or margin planning explicitly, the same way a marketplace commission should be priced in. A gateway fee that looks small per transaction adds up at volume, and a seller who prices as if digital payments are free is quietly eroding margin on every digital-payment order.

Combining COD and digital payments instead of choosing one

Dropping COD entirely is rarely the right move for a consumer-facing store in Pakistan — a meaningful share of buyers still prefer or only trust paying on delivery, especially for a first purchase from an unfamiliar store. The better approach is offering both at checkout and nudging behavior over time: a small discount or priority dispatch for prepaid orders, a request to confirm larger COD orders by phone before shipping, and tracking which payment method correlates with lower return and refusal rates for your specific customer base. Over time, repeat customers who've had a good first experience are far more likely to choose prepaid, which reduces the COD refusal cost that eats into margin on a cash-only store.

Security and compliance basics

A hosted checkout or a well-maintained plugin keeps the store from ever directly handling card numbers or wallet credentials, which meaningfully reduces its security and compliance burden compared with building a custom payment form. If a custom integration does touch card data directly, it needs to meet PCI-DSS requirements, which is a significant undertaking most small businesses are better off avoiding by sticking to a hosted or gateway-managed checkout instead.

Keep basic fraud controls in place regardless of gateway: confirm high-value orders before dispatch, watch for repeated failed-payment attempts from the same device or card, and keep transaction records organized — both for your own reconciliation and because the State Bank of Pakistan's payment system regulations and each gateway's own merchant agreement set requirements around recordkeeping and dispute handling that are worth reading in full before going live, since they're updated periodically.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Applying to every gateway at once. Verification and testing for even one provider takes real time — spreading that effort across three gateways simultaneously usually means none of them get tested properly before launch.
  • Skipping sandbox testing. A failed payment, a timeout, or a duplicate charge that isn't tested before go-live becomes a customer complaint instead of a caught bug.
  • Pricing as if the gateway fee doesn't exist. It's a real cost on every digital transaction and belongs in the margin calculation from day one.
  • Dropping COD before digital trust is established. Most Pakistani consumer stores still need COD as an option for a meaningful share of buyers, particularly new customers.
  • Assuming fee and settlement terms from an old article or a competitor's experience. Rates and timelines change — confirm current terms directly with the gateway or bank before pricing products around them.

Conclusion

JazzCash, Easypaisa, and bank-based gateways each open up a different slice of Pakistan's digital payment landscape — mobile wallet users, card holders, and higher-ticket buyers, respectively. Start with one gateway and the simplest integration method that fits your platform, test thoroughly in sandbox before going live, and keep cash on delivery available alongside it rather than replacing it outright. The goal isn't picking the "best" gateway — it's giving customers the payment method they already trust while gradually shifting more orders toward prepaid, where margins are healthier and refusal rates disappear.
