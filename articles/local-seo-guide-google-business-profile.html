Local SEO: How to Rank in Google's Map Pack and Get Found by Nearby Customers

Local SEO is a different discipline from general SEO — it's won or lost on your Google Business Profile, citation consistency, and reviews. Here's what actually moves the needle for a business that depends on nearby customers.

If your business serves customers in a specific city or a handful of neighborhoods — a dentist, an HVAC company, a law firm, a restaurant, a salon — general SEO advice only gets you partway there. The searches that actually bring in customers look like "plumber near me" or "family dentist in [city]," and those queries trigger a different set of ranking mechanics than a purely informational search does. This guide covers what local SEO actually involves: the profile you don't control the layout of but do control the content of, the unglamorous consistency work that underpins it, and the review and link signals that separate businesses that show up in the map pack from ones that don't.

What local SEO actually is

Local SEO is the practice of optimizing your online presence so you show up when people nearby search for what you offer. It overlaps with traditional SEO — content quality and technical basics still matter — but it adds a set of location-specific signals that traditional SEO doesn't weigh at all: your Google Business Profile, how consistently your business is listed across the web, and how customers rate and review you.

The clearest sign you're dealing with a local search is Google's response to it: instead of ten blue links, you get a "map pack" — a block of three business listings with a map, ratings, and a "more places" link beneath it. Ranking in that map pack, not just in the regular organic results below it, is the actual goal of local SEO, because it's where the overwhelming majority of local search clicks go.

Google Business Profile: the foundation

Google Business Profile (formerly Google My Business) is the single most important local SEO asset you have, and it's free. It's also the one place where an under-optimized profile is purely a self-inflicted problem — everything on it is within your control.

1

Claim and verify your profile

Search your business name on Google Maps; if a listing already exists (often auto-generated), claim it. Verification is usually done by postcard, phone, or email, depending on your business type.

2

Fill in every field completely

Exact business name (no keyword-stuffing it), correct category and secondary categories, service area or address, hours including holiday hours, phone number, and website URL. Incomplete profiles rank worse and convert worse.

3

Write a real business description

Describe what you do and who you serve in plain language. Avoid keyword stuffing — Google's guidelines explicitly discourage it and profiles that violate them can be suspended.

4

Add photos regularly

Listings with more photos get more clicks and direction requests. Add real photos of your storefront, team, and work — not stock imagery.

5

Use Posts and Q&A

Google Posts (updates, offers, events) and proactively answering your own Q&A section with useful information both keep the profile active, which correlates with better visibility.

NAP consistency and why it matters

NAP stands for Name, Address, Phone number — the three identifying details that need to match, exactly, everywhere your business appears online: your website, your Google Business Profile, social media profiles, and any directory or citation site. "123 Main St, Suite 4" and "123 Main Street #4" are inconsistent in Google's eyes, even though a human reads them as identical.

Inconsistent NAP data doesn't just look unprofessional — it makes it harder for Google to confidently associate all those mentions with the same business, which weakens the trust signal that consistent citations are supposed to build. Before doing anything else, write down the exact, canonical version of your NAP and use it everywhere, character for character.

Building citations the right way

A citation is any online mention of your business's NAP, whether or not it links to your site — think Yelp, Apple Maps, industry-specific directories, chambers of commerce, and local news mentions. Citations function as a trust signal: the more consistent, legitimate places that agree your business exists at a given address with a given phone number, the more confident Google is in showing you for local queries.

  • Start with the major data aggregators and platforms — Google Business Profile, Bing Places, Apple Maps, Yelp, and Facebook cover the bulk of citation value for most businesses.
  • Add industry-specific directories — a law firm benefits from legal directories, a contractor from trade-specific ones. These carry more relevance weight than generic directories.
  • Don't chase volume for its own sake. A handful of accurate, relevant citations outperforms dozens of low-quality directory submissions with inconsistent data.
  • Audit periodically. If your business moves or changes its phone number, old citations with outdated information actively work against you — track down and correct them.

Reviews: getting them and responding to them

Review count, review recency, and average rating are all direct inputs into map pack ranking — and reviews are also the single biggest factor in whether a potential customer actually calls once they find you. Neither getting reviews nor responding to them can be treated as optional.

The most reliable way to get reviews is also the simplest: ask, at the moment a customer's satisfaction is highest — right after a job is completed or a purchase is picked up — and make it easy with a direct link to your Google review form. Never offer incentives or discounts in exchange for reviews; it violates Google's policies and, in most jurisdictions, consumer protection law, and the practice is easy for platforms to detect at scale.

Responding to reviews matters almost as much as getting them. Reply to positive reviews with a genuine, brief thank-you, and to negative ones calmly and specifically — acknowledge the issue, and where appropriate, describe what you did or will do about it. Prospective customers read how a business handles criticism more closely than they read the criticism itself.

On-site local SEO signals

Your Google Business Profile carries a lot of weight, but your website still needs to reinforce the same signals:

  • NAP on your website — typically in the footer and on a contact page, matching your canonical NAP exactly.
  • Location pages for multi-area businesses — a genuinely distinct page per service area, with unique content about that location, not a template with the city name swapped in.
  • Local schema markup — LocalBusiness structured data helps search engines parse your address, hours, and service area programmatically, and can unlock richer search appearances.
  • Location-aware content — naturally mentioning the neighborhoods, landmarks, or service areas you actually serve, without stuffing city names into every sentence.

Local link building and community involvement

Backlinks from other local businesses, organizations, and publications carry particular weight for local rankings because they reinforce that you're a real, active participant in that geographic community — not just a listing. Practical, realistic ways to earn them:

  • Sponsor a local sports team, school event, or charity fundraiser — most will link to sponsors on their site.
  • Join your local chamber of commerce or a relevant trade association; most list members with a link.
  • Partner with complementary (non-competing) local businesses for cross-promotion — a caterer and an event venue, for example.
  • Pitch local news outlets or community blogs with a genuinely newsworthy angle, not a press release about routine business operations.

What actually drives map pack rankings

Google doesn't publish its exact local ranking algorithm, but based on documented guidance and consistent field results, the following factors are the ones worth prioritizing:

FactorWhy it matters
ProximityHow close the searcher is to your location — the one factor you can't influence directly, which is why the rest matter more
RelevanceHow well your profile's category and content match the search query's intent
ProminenceOverall business reputation, informed by review volume/rating and general web presence
Profile completenessFully filled-out profiles with accurate categories and attributes outperform sparse ones
Review signalsRating, review count, review recency, and whether the business responds to reviews
Citation consistencyAccurate, matching NAP data across major platforms and relevant directories

Multi-location businesses

If you operate more than one location, each one needs its own Google Business Profile and, ideally, its own unique page on your website — never one profile listing multiple addresses, and never duplicate location pages that differ only by city name. Each location's content should reflect what's actually distinct about it: the team, specific services offered there, and genuine local context. Thin, templated location pages tend to get filtered out of search results entirely rather than simply ranking poorly.

Common local SEO mistakes

  • Keyword-stuffing the business name field — appending a service or city to your Google Business Profile name violates Google's guidelines and risks suspension.
  • Inconsistent NAP data — old addresses or phone numbers left uncorrected across directories.
  • Ignoring negative reviews — an unanswered bad review reads worse than a calmly handled one.
  • Duplicate or templated location pages — content that doesn't meaningfully differ between service areas.
  • Buying fake reviews or review incentives — a policy violation that can get a profile suspended outright, wiping out its entire review history.

Measuring local SEO results

Google Business Profile's built-in insights show how customers found you (direct search vs. discovery search), what actions they took (calls, direction requests, website clicks), and how your photos and posts are performing. Beyond that, track your ranking position for your priority local queries using rank-tracking tools that support location-based searches, since your position from your own office can differ meaningfully from what a customer three miles away actually sees. As with organic SEO generally, local rankings move over weeks and months, not days — consistent citation and review-generation work compounds rather than producing immediate jumps.

Conclusion

Local SEO rewards unglamorous consistency more than clever tactics: a fully completed Google Business Profile, NAP data that matches everywhere it appears, a steady stream of genuine reviews you actually respond to, and a website that reinforces the same location signals. None of it is complicated, but all of it needs to be done accurately and kept current — which is exactly why so many local businesses that do it well end up with a durable advantage over competitors who never got past claiming their listing.
