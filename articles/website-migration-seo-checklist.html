Website Migration SEO Checklist: How to Change Domains or Platforms Without Losing Rankings

A rebrand, a new domain, or a switch to a different CMS can wipe out years of accumulated rankings in a single afternoon — not because migrations are inherently risky, but because most of them skip the steps that make them safe.

A "migration" covers more ground than people usually mean by it. Moving from an old domain to a new one after a rebrand is a migration. Switching from WordPress to Shopify, or from a custom-built site to a modern framework, is a migration. Consolidating three regional sites into one, changing from HTTP to HTTPS, restructuring URLs to remove date stamps, or even a redesign that quietly changes internal linking — all of these carry the same risk: search engines have to relearn which pages exist, where they live, and whether they're still the pages worth ranking. Get that relearning process wrong and traffic that took years to build can drop 30-60% within weeks, sometimes permanently.

None of this means migrations should be avoided. Rebrands happen, platforms get outgrown, and sometimes the old site is genuinely holding the business back. The risk is manageable — migrations that preserve rankings and migrations that tank them differ almost entirely in preparation, not luck. This checklist walks through that preparation in the order it actually needs to happen, from the planning that happens weeks before launch to the monitoring that continues weeks after.

Why migrations actually go wrong

Nearly every migration disaster traces back to one of four root causes, and it's worth naming them before the checklist because they explain why each step below exists:

  • URLs change without redirects. Every backlink pointing at an old URL, and every ranking signal search engines associated with it, goes to a 404 instead of transferring to the new page.
  • Content gets lost or thinned in translation. A new CMS or a redesign drops body copy, FAQ sections, or internal links that were quietly doing ranking work nobody remembers assigning to them.
  • The new site gets launched before search engines are told about it. No updated sitemap, no reindex request, and Google discovers the change on its own schedule instead of being pointed at it.
  • Nobody checks the new environment is actually crawlable. A staging-site noindex tag, a forgotten Disallow: / in robots.txt, or broken canonical tags carried over from a staging build are the single most common cause of a migration that "goes fine" and then rankings never come back — because the new site was invisible to Google from day one.

Everything in this checklist exists to catch one of those four problems before it reaches production.

Before you touch anything: decide what's actually changing

Not every migration carries the same risk, and knowing which kind you're running determines how much of this checklist applies at full weight.

Migration typeWhat changesRelative risk
Domain change (same content, same URLs otherwise)Domain name onlyModerate — handled almost entirely by redirects and Search Console's Change of Address tool
Platform/CMS change, URLs preservedUnderlying system, template, hostingModerate — risk concentrated in content fidelity and technical setup, not URL mapping
URL structure changePath structure, slugs, or site architectureHigh — every changed URL needs an individual redirect and a reason for Google to trust the new one
Full replatform + domain change + redesignEverything at onceHighest — combines every risk above and makes it hardest to isolate what broke if something does

If it's realistic, avoid changing the domain, URL structure, and platform all in the same launch. Doing them in sequence — platform first, then a later domain change once the new platform is stable — makes it far easier to diagnose a problem, because there's only one variable that could have caused it.

Build a complete URL mapping spreadsheet

This is the single most important artifact in any migration, and the step most often done partially. The goal is a row for every URL on the live site, mapped to exactly where it lives after launch.

  1. Export every indexed URL. Pull the full list from your XML sitemap, a site crawl (Screaming Frog's free tier handles up to 500 URLs), and Google Search Console's Page Indexing report — cross-reference all three, because sitemaps often miss pages a crawl finds and vice versa.
  2. Note which URLs actually earn traffic or links. Pull the last 12 months of landing page data from GA4 and your backlink profile (Search Console's Links report is free and sufficient for most small sites). These are the URLs where a mistake does the most damage.
  3. Map each old URL to its new equivalent, one-to-one wherever possible. A blog post that becomes a different blog post at a different URL should redirect to that specific post — not to the new blog's homepage. Redirecting everything to a homepage or category page ("redirect dumping") preserves almost none of the original ranking value.
  4. Flag pages with no real equivalent. A discontinued product or a service you no longer offer doesn't need a forced redirect to something unrelated — redirecting it to the closest genuinely relevant page, or letting it 404 cleanly if nothing fits, is more honest than mapping it to an unrelated page just to avoid a 404.

Set up 301 redirects correctly

A 301 ("moved permanently") redirect is what tells search engines to transfer a URL's accumulated ranking signals to its new address instead of starting over. Getting this step wrong undoes almost everything else on this list.

  • Use 301s, not 302s. A 302 ("moved temporarily") tells search engines the move might not be permanent, so they're slower to transfer ranking signals and may keep indexing the old URL in parallel.
  • Redirect every old URL individually to its specific new equivalent. Pattern-based redirects (e.g. stripping a date segment from every blog URL with one rule) work well when the URL structure change is systematic, but verify the pattern actually produces the right destination for every URL it touches — test a sample, not just the rule logic.
  • Avoid redirect chains. If the old URL redirects to an intermediate URL that itself redirects to the final destination, collapse it to a single hop. Chains dilute the signal passed along and slow down every crawl and every visitor.
  • Redirect the non-canonical variants too. www vs. non-www, trailing slash vs. no trailing slash, and HTTP vs. HTTPS should all resolve to one canonical version on the new site, exactly as they should have on the old one.
  • Keep redirects live for at least 12 months after launch. Search engines re-crawl at different rates, and some backlinks — especially older ones — may not get followed again for months. Removing redirects early orphans any traffic or authority still routed through them.

Preserve content and technical parity

A redirect gets a visitor and a search engine to the right address. It doesn't guarantee the page they land on is still worth ranking. This is the step a redesign or replatform most often shortcuts, because it's tempting to treat the migration as a chance to "clean up" content without checking what that cleanup actually removes.

  • Carry over title tags, meta descriptions, and header structure on pages that were already ranking well — a new template shouldn't mean every page gets a generic new title.
  • Preserve internal linking. Internal links are how authority flows between pages on a site, and a new template or navigation often drops links that used to point at specific deep pages. Audit the old site's internal link structure before launch and recreate the links that mattered, not just the ones the new template happens to generate automatically.
  • Don't thin out content "for the new design." FAQ sections, long-form body copy, and embedded tables are frequently cut during a redesign because they look dense in a new template — and frequently the exact content Google was ranking the page for.
  • Carry over structured data. Article, Product, FAQPage, and LocalBusiness schema markup (see our schema markup guide) doesn't migrate automatically with a platform change and has to be rebuilt and re-validated on the new site.
  • Re-verify Core Web Vitals on the new platform. A new CMS or theme can quietly introduce layout shift or slower load times even when visually nothing looks different — run PageSpeed Insights against the new templates before launch, not after.

Validate on staging before launch

This is the step that catches the "invisible to Google from day one" failure mode, and it has to happen before the new site goes live, not after.

  • Block search engines from the staging environment itself with a password or IP restriction — not just a noindex tag, which can accidentally survive the move to production if nobody remembers to remove it.
  • Crawl the staging site with the same tool used for the URL audit and confirm every migrated URL returns a 200, every internal link points to a live page, and canonical tags point to the new domain, not the old one.
  • Check robots.txt on staging and compare it against what production should ship with. A staging robots.txt with Disallow: / getting pushed live by mistake is one of the most common — and most completely invisible — migration failures.
  • Test a sample of redirects directly by visiting old URLs against the staging or pre-launch server and confirming they land on the correct new page with a 301, not a 302 or a 404.

Launch day: the sequence that matters

  1. Deploy during low-traffic hours and have the old site's final state backed up and ready to restore if something goes seriously wrong.
  2. Confirm redirects are live first by testing the same URL sample used in staging validation, now against production.
  3. Remove any staging-only blocks — password protection, IP restrictions, or lingering noindex tags — immediately after confirming the redirects work, not before.
  4. Submit the new XML sitemap in Google Search Console and, for a domain change specifically, use Search Console's Change of Address tool, which exists precisely for this scenario and speeds up how quickly Google re-associates the old domain's signals with the new one.
  5. Request indexing on your 10-15 highest-value URLs manually through Search Console's URL Inspection tool rather than waiting for a full recrawl to reach them naturally.
  6. Update Google Business Profile, social profiles, and any directory listings that link to the old domain, where that's realistic to do quickly — these are often overlooked and can keep sending traffic to dead URLs for months.

Post-launch monitoring: the first 90 days

Rankings don't transfer instantly even on a clean migration — a dip in the first two to four weeks while Google reprocesses the site is normal and not itself a sign of failure. What matters is whether that dip recovers.

  • Check Search Console's Page Indexing report weekly for the first month. Watch specifically for a spike in "Not found (404)" or "Page with redirect" exclusions — both indicate redirect mapping gaps that need fixing immediately, not a wait-and-see problem.
  • Track organic traffic and keyword rankings against pre-migration baselines rather than against the same period last year, since a migration's impact shows up fastest in week-over-week comparisons.
  • Watch Core Web Vitals in Search Console for regressions that didn't show up in staging testing but appear under real traffic load.
  • If a specific page hasn't recovered after 60-90 days, check its redirect individually, confirm its content wasn't thinned relative to the old version, and verify it's still getting internal links from elsewhere on the new site.
  • A migration that still hasn't substantially recovered by 90 days usually points to a specific unresolved technical issue — a redirect gap, a crawlability block, or lost content — rather than something that will fix itself with more time.

Full checklist, in order

  1. Decide exactly what's changing (domain, platform, URLs, or a combination) and sequence unrelated changes separately where possible.
  2. Export every existing URL from the sitemap, a crawl, and Search Console, and identify which earn traffic or backlinks.
  3. Build a one-to-one URL mapping spreadsheet from old to new addresses.
  4. Set up individual 301 redirects for every mapped URL, avoiding chains and redirect dumping.
  5. Preserve titles, headers, internal links, body content, and structured data on pages that were already ranking.
  6. Re-run Core Web Vitals checks against the new templates.
  7. Validate the full site on staging: crawl for broken links, confirm robots.txt and canonical tags, test redirect samples.
  8. Launch during low traffic, confirm redirects first, then remove staging blocks.
  9. Submit the new sitemap, use Change of Address if the domain changed, and manually request indexing on top pages.
  10. Monitor Search Console weekly for 30 days, then biweekly through day 90, and investigate any page that hasn't recovered by then individually.

Conclusion

A website migration isn't inherently a gamble with years of accumulated SEO. The sites that lose rankings during a migration are almost always missing one of the same handful of steps — a redirect map built from guesswork instead of a real audit, content thinned out in a redesign, or a staging block that made it to production by accident. Work through this checklist in order, validate on staging before launch rather than after, and keep watching the numbers for the first 90 days. Done this way, a migration is a one-time project with a predictable short dip, not an open-ended risk to the traffic the old site took years to earn.
